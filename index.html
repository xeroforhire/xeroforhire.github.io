About

Jashae Slaughter — known online as Xeroforhire for more than 14 years — is a writer, podcaster, musician, and independent creator. His work spans speculative fiction, comics, music, and cultural commentary, all originating from the same hand and belonging to one larger body of work.

He is the founder of the Apocalyptiverse, his independent press, and the creator of its flagship sci-fi action thriller The Last Day. He hosts The Xero for Hire Podcast and records music as The Legend of XERO.

Creative Philosophy

Slaughter writes from a Christian worldview, but the worldview is the lens, not the message. His fiction is deliberately anti-didactic: most cyberpunk trends nihilistic, while his work assumes a moral backbone exists and asks how people actually operate inside worlds like ours, but fantastical. Story comes first; theme is embedded rather than applied to the surface.

Across the commentary and the creative work alike, the throughline is discernment over tribe — testing ideas against evidence and a coherent worldview rather than defaulting to what any given audience expects to hear.

Background

The Xeroforhire name has been Slaughter's creative identity for over a decade — the umbrella under which he publishes, podcasts, and makes music. This site exists to gather that work in one place, owned and maintained directly, so the full picture survives independent of any platform's algorithm or longevity.

Media FAQ

What is the Apocalyptiverse?

The independent press and the universe under which all of Jashae Slaughter's fiction lives — the hub for his worldbuilding across novels, comics, and short fiction. The Last Day is the flagship.

Why cyberpunk and sci-fi from a Christian worldview?

Most cyberpunk is nihilistic and often explicitly anti-Christian. The Apocalyptiverse is built within a Christian worldview framework instead — not as instruction, but as speculation: how would people actually operate in circumstances like ours, but fantastical? It assumes a moral backbone exists without lecturing about it.

Is this didactic fiction?

No. The stories aren't written to teach or preach. Faith is tested and refined through struggle in the narrative — never bolted on as a message.

What does "irreverent Christian perspective" mean?

Tackling complex and sometimes controversial topics with honesty and humor while holding a foundational belief in Christian truth — a high view of Scripture paired with a low tolerance for performative outrage and cultural conformity on any side.

What sets the commentary apart?

A willingness to turn the same scrutiny on his own side as on anyone else's. The throughline isn't a political team; it's divergent thinking and discernment — follow the evidence and the worldview, not the tribe. Listeners looking only for agreement are told, plainly, that they may not be the right audience for the show.

How does he think about AI?

As a tool for stewardship, not a moral agent. The real question is discernment: use AI for rote tasks, but reserve conceptual leadership for the human. He draws a sharp line between convergent thinking, which AI does well, and divergent thinking — the uniquely human capacity he sees as central to real artistry. The danger isn't the tool; it's accepting its outputs uncritically and drifting into algorithmic conformity.

How does he think about "Christian" music?

Skeptically, as a category. Labeling art "Christian" too often lowers the bar, prioritizing message over craft. The goal is excellent work on its own merits — truth and beauty aren't confined to a genre, and worship is a way of living rather than a musical style.
